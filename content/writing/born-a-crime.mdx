Rachel Savage, in The Guardian on 29 March 2026, reports that Graaff-Reinet, a South African town founded in 1786, is being renamed after Robert Sobukwe, the Pan Africanist Congress founder who led the 1960 protests against the pass laws. A December 2023 Stellenbosch survey found 83.6% of residents opposed. By race, opposition ran 92.9% among Coloured people, 98.5% among white people, and 55% among Black people. Apartheid, the article says, "lumped together as Coloured" people who were mixed-race, Khoi-San, and descended from the enslaved, and Coloured people "were allowed slightly better houses and jobs, forcing them to distance themselves from Black people to access those benefits." One resident, Laughton Hoffman, says the anger is neglect since 1994, not the old order.

I read that 92.9% the way we read a scar and already know the shape of the blade. The survey pretends to photograph a town. It hands each person one drawer, the race drawer, and sweeps the rest of them onto the floor. A whole life, folded away as nothing. Then it counts the drawers and calls it an opinion. This is the oldest courtesy I know. It renames the town after a freedom fighter while the land keeps its owners and the jobs keep theirs, and it holds the mirror up to people’s faces and calls the reflection fair. Only the signboard is repainted, but the hunger underneath still keeps its old address.

Hoffman is not lying. His pain is real and I will not walk over it. Even 55% of Black residents opposed the rename, so here the anger isn’t only about apartheid’s buffer. Still, the gap matters: 55% compared with 92.9%. That extra distance is where the buffer shows. But there is a kind of truth a cage teaches us so gently that in the end we mistake the bars for our own bones. The buffer never felt like a buffer; it felt like a better roof, like a wage that came on Fridays.

I know this drawer from the inside. I grew up in Bangladesh and came to study in the United States. That is why I have another name provided here. I am called an alien, and this is not something individual. So, I should rather say, we are called aliens, where a person is handed one column of themselves and told the rest does not count, and you learn to guard the small thing they let you keep, because it is the only thing with your name still on it. So when I read that 92.9%, I did not see hatred. I saw people holding the one coin apartheid pressed into their palm, afraid of the empty hand underneath.

Born a Crime is a true map of that fear, I have found. Noah was born from a relationship the law called a crime, so he could not walk freely with his own family. When police came near, his mother would let go of his hand and his father walked on the far side of the street, because a light child beside them was proof of the offense. In "The Mulberry Tree," Noah lays the wiring bare. Coloured people were the buffer, the amperbaas, the "almost-boss." There was the pencil test. "Some colored people would get promoted to white." And, flatly, "the benchmark is black." That is the whole survey in four words. If your worth is only your distance from Blackness, then refusing a Black man’s name on your town is not hate. It is the day job. Noah writes that "for all that black people have suffered, they know who they are. Colored people don’t." The buffer was built to own no ground, only a gap it must forever fall through.

The epigraph to his first chapter names the design. "The genius of apartheid was convincing people who were the overwhelming majority to turn on each other... You separate people into groups and make them hate one another so you can run them all." Just like that, it still works, and Graaff-Reinet’s 92.9% is that sentence. Sometimes I feel like this whole world system is a machine, and the machine needs no apartheid now. It needs only the old boxes and someone to ask the question inside them. The town gets a new name every time and the row stays sorted. Loyalty was never the feeling. It was the wage.
